Navionics Raised Prices (Again) and Killed Its Web Version — Here's What Anglers Are Switching To

If You're Here Because of the Price Hike, You're Not Alone

Maybe you opened the Navionics app last month and noticed your renewal went up. Maybe you tried to plan a route on your laptop and discovered the web version no longer exists. Or maybe you saw the Reddit thread titled "Navionics Price Increase Again" and started doing the math on what you're actually getting for $49.99 a year.

All three happened in 2026. Here's the timeline:

  • The web version was shut down. Navionics confirmed the shutdown — route planning now lives exclusively inside the Boating app, behind an active subscription. If you liked planning trips on a big screen, that's gone.
  • Prices went up — repeatedly. Navionics doubled its subscription in late 2023 (from $24.99 to $49.99), and the r/sailing thread "Navionics Price Increase Again" documents further increases on top of that.
  • Longstanding issues persist. App Store reviews mention iPhone display problems that still aren't resolved.

Navionics is owned by Garmin, and this pattern — fewer surfaces, higher prices — is classic margin-squeeze behavior from a corporate parent. It's not evil. It's just business. But it's also a perfectly reasonable moment to ask: what are my options, and what am I actually paying for?

That's what this post answers. I'll be honest about what Navionics does well, break down the alternatives by what you actually need, and tell you plainly where my own app (FishSmart Pro) fits — and where it doesn't.

First: What Navionics Actually Does Well

Credit where it's due. Navionics builds genuinely excellent bathymetric charts:

  • SonarChart — 1-foot contour maps generated from user-contributed sonar logs. For reading structure, nothing free comes close.
  • Community edits — crowdsourced hazards, bottom composition, and marina data that update constantly.
  • Navionics+ layers — relief shading and satellite overlay for scouting.
  • Plotter integration — if you run a Garmin chartplotter, the subscription syncs your cards and data.

If you're a saltwater boater navigating unfamiliar water, or a Great Lakes angler who lives on contour detail, Navionics is a serious tool. This post isn't "Navionics bad." It's "your subscription just got more expensive while delivering less — here's how to think about the alternatives."

The real question isn't Navionics vs. everything else. It's: which tool matches your actual use case at a price that makes sense?

What Are You Actually Paying For? Match the Tool to the Job

Most anglers who balk at the price hike fall into one of four camps. Find yours:

1. "I mainly fish my home lake and just want to know WHEN to go"

You don't need $50/year of bathymetry for water you already know. You need to know whether Thursday evening or Saturday morning is the better bet — and why.

That's a forecasting problem, not a mapping problem. FishSmart Pro ($29.99/yr) is built exactly for this: a bite score calculated from live USGS water temperature, barometric pressure trends, dissolved oxygen, thermocline depth, moon phase, and photoperiod — with every factor shown and explained. If the pressure is crashing and the wind is right, you'll see the score climb and know why.

2. "I need maps and contours for new water"

Then you need a mapping tool — and here the honest answer is that FishSmart Pro is not a Navionics replacement, and I won't pretend it is. It doesn't do bathymetric charts. Your best options:

  • onX Fish ($34.99/yr) — lake discovery with DNR species data, 3D lake maps, satellite imagery, and offline GPS across 12 Midwest states. The closest thing to a modern Navionics competitor for freshwater discovery.
  • Your chartplotter's built-in mapping — if you already own a Garmin/Lowrance/Humminbird unit, check what you get before double-subscribing.
  • Free government resources — many state DNRs publish lake contour PDFs for free. Ugly, but free.

3. "I want the fishing community / social angle"

Fishbrain ($74.99–79.99/yr) owns this niche — catch logs, shared spots, social feeds. Just know that their free tier is actively shrinking as features move behind the Pro paywall, and their pricing varies by platform ($9.99/mo on Android vs. $59.99/yr on web at various points — the inconsistency is its own trust problem).

Also know: social fishing apps run on shared catch locations. If that's your thing, enjoy it. If the thought of your best spot appearing on a heat map makes you uneasy, it's a reason to stay away.

What to Do Before Your Next Renewal

Whether your renewal lands next month or next spring, the price trajectory is clear. Here's a quick framework:

  1. Audit what you actually used last year. Open the app and check. If you never touched SonarChart edits or plotter sync, you were paying for capability you didn't use.
  2. Check your state's free contour resources. Many anglers discover their DNR already publishes the contours they were subscribing to.
  3. Identify which job you're replacing. Mapping → onX Fish or plotter mapping. Forecasting → FishSmart Pro. Social → Fishbrain (with eyes open).
  4. Test alternatives during your current subscription. You don't have to quit anything today. Run a comparison for a few weekends.

The Bottom Line

Navionics didn't become a bad product. It became a more expensive one with fewer access points, and that changes the value math for a lot of anglers. The smart move in 2026 isn't finding one app that does everything — it's matching each job to the best tool:

  • Where: onX Fish ($34.99) or your chartplotter
  • When + why: FishSmart Pro ($29.99)
  • Contours, saltwater navigation: Navionics — still excellent, just pricier
  • Social: Fishbrain — if the paywall carousel hasn't worn you out
